Glossary — the language of VPA

Every term you'll meet in this guide, in one place. Skim it now, then refer back whenever a word trips you up.

Price
Where a market is trading — the direction of a move.
Volume
How many shares/contracts changed hands — the effort or crowd behind a move.
Spread
The height of a candle's body (open‑to‑close). Wide = strong conviction; narrow = indecision.
Wick (shadow/tail)
The thin line above/below the body — price that was reached then rejected. Marks a change of sentiment.
Validation
Volume agrees with price (e.g. rising price + rising volume). The move is genuine — carry on.
Anomaly
Volume contradicts price (e.g. big move on tiny volume). An alarm bell — investigate.
Effort vs. result
Wyckoff's core test: compare volume (effort) to spread (result). They should be in harmony.
Accumulation
Insiders quietly buying at low prices over a sideways range, using bad news to scare sellers out.
Distribution
Insiders quietly selling at high prices into a greedy public near a top.
Testing
A dip back into an old zone to check if supply/demand is gone. Low volume = safe; high volume = it's back.
Buying climax
Insiders buying at a bottom as the public panic‑sells (high volume, lower wicks).
Selling climax
Insiders selling at a top as the public buys euphorically (high volume, upper wicks).
Stopping volume
High volume with deep lower wicks that brakes a falling market over several candles.
Topping‑out volume
High volume with deep upper wicks that brakes a rising market over several candles.
Shooting star
Small body, long upper wick = weakness (rejected higher prices).
Hammer
Small body, long lower wick = strength (rejected lower prices, "hammering out a bottom").
Long‑legged doji
Long wicks both sides, open ≈ close = pure indecision.
Support / Resistance
A price floor (support) and ceiling (resistance) formed by trapped fear/greed.
Isolated pivot
A 3‑candle reversal: the middle candle has the highest high (pivot high) or lowest low (pivot low).
Breakout / Fake‑out
Price escaping congestion. On strong volume = real breakout; on low volume = a fake‑out trap.
VPA
Volume Price Analysis — reading volume over time against price (the outside view).
VAP
Volume At Price — a side histogram of volume at each price level (the inside view).
Smart money / Insiders
Market makers, banks and big operators who must transact in size — and so leave footprints in volume.
Cause & effect
Wyckoff's law: the bigger the "cause" (consolidation), the bigger the "effect" (the move that follows).
Tick volume
A count of price changes used as a volume proxy in forex (~90% representative of real activity).

Golden rule: volume confirms price. When the two disagree, the market is telling you something — stop and ask why.

The original idea

One of Dow's principle beliefs was that volume confirmed trends in price. He maintained that if a price was moving on low volume, then there could be many different reasons. However, when a price move was associated with high or rising volume, then he believed this was a valid move. If the price continued moving in one direction, and with associated supporting volume, then this was the signal of the start of a trend.

In simple terms

Think of price as where a stock is going, and volume as how many people agree with that move.

📈 Price

The direction — up or down.

👥 Volume

The crowd — how many shares traded.

A price move backed by big or growing volume is like a march with a huge crowd — it's serious and likely to keep going. A move on low volume is like a few people wandering — it could mean anything, so don't trust it as a real trend.

Strong move vs. weak move

VALID TREND

Price rises and volume rises → the crowd agrees. Trust it.

WEAK / SUSPECT

Price rises but volume fades → few agree. Be cautious.

How to read these two charts

Left (valid): the green line is price climbing left‑to‑right. The green bars along the bottom are volume — one bar per session — and they get taller as price rises. Price and volume are moving together, so the effort (volume) matches the result (the price gain). This is a real move: the big players are buying into it, and you can trust it to continue.

Right (suspect): the price line rises in the exact same way — if you only looked at price, the two charts would seem identical. But the red volume bars are shrinking. Price is going up while participation is drying up. That mismatch is the warning: nobody of size is behind this move, so it is likely to stall or reverse.

Why it matters: this single comparison is the entire book in a nutshell. Price alone cannot tell these two situations apart — only volume can. A rising market should be fuelled by rising volume; when it isn't, something is wrong.

How a trend begins

Direction + supporting volume, sustained over time = the start of a real trend.

Reading the flow

A trend needs all three boxes, in order. First price has to actually move one way (not just chop sideways). Second, that move must be backed by rising volume — proof the crowd is joining in, not just a random flicker. Only when both hold, and keep holding for more than a candle or two, do you have the third box: a genuine trend worth trading.

If the middle box is missing (price moves but volume doesn't rise), you don't get to the third box — you get a fake‑out instead.

The three stages of a trend

From this basic principle, Dow developed the idea that every trend moves through three stages. The chart below shows a full bullish cycle, with volume telling the real story underneath.

1. Accumulation

The starting point of the move higher. The smart money quietly buys while others aren't paying attention.

2. Public Participation

The trend-following stage — usually the longest. The wider market notices and joins in.

3. Distribution

Investors rush in, terrified of missing out. Meanwhile the smart money is doing the opposite — taking profits and selling to the eager crowd.

Walking through the chart

The white line is price over one full cycle. Read it left to right in three coloured bands:

  1. Blue band — Accumulation. Price is flat and low. Smart money is quietly buying the shares that frightened sellers are dumping. Volume (the little bars) is modest because they're careful not to push price up too soon.
  2. Green band — Public Participation. Price climbs steadily. The wider public notices the uptrend and piles in. Volume grows as more and more traders join. This is the longest stage and where most of the visible "trend" happens.
  3. Red band — Distribution. Price tops out in a rounded curve. Volume is at its heaviest — but that's not strength, it's the smart money selling their whole inventory to a euphoric public that's terrified of missing out. Once they're done, price rolls over.

Notice the trap: the public feels most confident (heaviest volume, highest price) at exactly the moment the professionals are heading for the exit. The whole point of VPA is to see that hand‑off happening — in the volume — before price falls.

Smart money vs. the public

The key insight: while the public is happily buying, the "smart money" is quietly selling and banking its profits. All of this buying and selling shows up in one place — volume.

🧠 Smart Money

Buys in accumulation (cheap, quiet).
Sells in distribution (expensive, crowded).

🐑 The Public

Mostly absent early on.
Rushes in late, buying at the top out of fear of missing out.

Volume is the prism through which all of this activity can be seen.

Wyckoff & the supply/demand engine

If Dow was the founding father of technical analysis, his contemporary Richard Wyckoff is the founding father of volume and price analysis — he built the method still used today. His core insight was simple: prices move on supply and demand, and by watching the price–volume relationship you can forecast direction.

Dow, Wyckoff, and Livermore all read the ticker tape through four elements: price, volume, time, and trend.

Wyckoff's three laws

From his work, Wyckoff distilled three basic laws — the working rules of volume price analysis.

1. The Law of Supply and Demand

When demand > supply, prices rise. When supply > demand, prices fall and the excess is absorbed. Think of the winter sales: prices drop, buyers come in and soak up the over‑supply.

2. The Law of Cause and Effect

No effect without a cause — and the effect is proportional to the cause. Small volume → small price move. Large volume → large trend. Like a wave at sea: a big wave rolls the ship violently, a small one barely moves it.

3. The Law of Effort vs. Result

Like Newton's third law: effort (volume) and result (price action) should be in harmony. Examine each bar forensically — if they agree, the market is healthy; if they don't, that's an anomaly to investigate, like a crime‑scene detective.

IN HARMONY

Rising price + rising volume → move on to next bar.

ANOMALY

Price rises but volume fades → why? Investigate.

Comparing the two mini charts

Both show the same rising price line — the difference is entirely in the volume bars beneath.

Left ("in harmony"): as price rises, the green volume bars grow taller. Effort and result agree, so the move is healthy. Wyckoff's advice: nothing to see here — move on to the next bar.

Right ("anomaly"): price rises the same way, but the red volume bars shrink. Big result, shrinking effort — the two disagree. That's the anomaly you stop and investigate, exactly like a detective who notices something that shouldn't be there.

This "effort vs. result" check, done bar by bar, is the single skill the whole book trains. Every later signal is really just a special case of "do effort and result agree — yes or no?"

Reading the ticker tape

For all these traders, the ticker tape was their window on the markets. Wyckoff called it a "method for forecasting from what appears on the tape now, what is likely to happen in the future."

Tape Reading is rapid‑fire horse sense. Its object is to determine whether stocks are being accumulated or distributed, marked up or down, or whether they are neglected by the large interests… It is gauging the momentary supply and demand in particular stocks and in the whole market, comparing the forces behind each and their relationship.

His analogy: a tape reader is like the manager of a department store, watching hundreds of sales reports pour in — noticing where demand is abnormally strong (restock, buy more) or weak (cut prices to tempt buyers). As traders, "surely this is all we need to know!"

Cracking the shorthand

The code was compressed for speed. A few key rules:

  • Top line = stock symbols — e.g. Goodyear GT, US Steel X, Radio Corp R, Westinghouse WX. PR = preferred (not common) stock.
  • Second line = prices & volumes.
  • S = separator between share count and price (like a dot).
  • SS = an odd lot — fewer than 100 shares.
  • Trailing zeros dropped for speed.

Worked example — US Steel (X), crash day

Early on the tape: 10,000 shares @ 185¾. By the end it reads 2.5½ = 200 shares — but on a day like that, was the price still 185, or had it crashed to 175 or 165? The tape's compression hid just how fast prices were falling.

What's changed since? Honestly, very little. Our charts are electronic and update tick‑by‑tick, but the information is the same: price, volume, trend, and time. Learn that relationship and the tape — paper or electronic — tells the same story.

Volume reveals the truth

Richard Ney gave us a sharp mental model: think of market specialists as merchants. They buy inventory at wholesale (accumulate cheaply from a scared public) and sell at retail (distribute expensively to an excited one) — then clear the shelves and repeat.

From this, Ney posited eight laws. In condensed form:

  • Specialists sell at retail what they bought at wholesale.
  • The longer they operate, the more capital they build to buy wholesale / sell retail.
  • More media → more participants → more volatility.
  • To move huge size, members use mass media to boost "sales".
  • Bigger resources need deeper declines to shake stock loose.
  • …and more dramatic rallies to attract the public for distribution.
  • The most active stocks take longest to distribute.
  • The economy suffers bigger breakdowns — inflation, unemployment, high rates, shortages.

Richard Ney called consecutive market tops and bottoms through the 1960s–80s — a scourge of the SEC and champion of the small investor.

The takeaway: because big operators must transact to accumulate and distribute, they leave footprints in volume. Stocks, forex, futures — market makers and major banks cannot hide. Volume validates price and exposes manipulation, order flow, supply/demand and sentiment in stark detail.

A little history

Charles Dow never published a formal book on trading. He shared his ideas in the early Wall Street Journal. Only after his death in 1902 was his work collected and published — first by his friend and colleague Sam Nelson, and later by William Hamilton.

The 1903 book The ABC of Stock Speculation was the first to use the term "Dow Theory" — a hook on which to hang the great man's ideas.

Reading the timeline

Three markers: Dow dies in 1902 without ever writing a trading book; the 1903 collection The ABC of Stock Speculation gathers his ideas; and the label "Dow Theory" is coined later to brand them.

The point: "Dow Theory" wasn't a system Dow packaged himself — it's a name others hung on his scattered Wall Street Journal writings after his death. The ideas (volume confirms price, trends have three stages) are what matter, not the branding.

Chapter Two — Why Volume?

"The key is having more information than the other guy — then analyzing it right and using it rationally."
— Warren Buffett

The Parable of Uncle Joe

Uncle Joe runs the only licensed widget distributor in the state. Plain buying and selling is dull and low‑margin — so he games it. His story is the whole market‑maker playbook in miniature:

Accumulate & mark up

Spread a "shortage" rumour, let demand build, then ratchet prices higher while the crowd keeps buying.

Distribute & mark down

When the shelves are empty, trigger fear ("a competitor!"), crash prices, and buy everything back cheap.

Accumulation → markup → distribution → markdown. Then: "I wonder if we could do that again?"

Following the curve

The white line is the widget price; read it left to right as Uncle Joe's scheme plays out:

  1. "Shortage" (rumour). Joe whispers that widgets are running low. Demand rises and price ticks up.
  2. Raise prices. With buyers hooked, he ratchets prices higher — and they keep buying because they fear missing out.
  3. Warehouse empties. At the top, Joe has quietly sold nearly all his stock. Notice buying volume secretly drops here — the tell that the move is exhausted.
  4. "Competitor!" (fear). Now he spreads bad news to scare everyone.
  5. Panic selling → buy back cheap. Prices crash as the crowd dumps, and Joe buys it all back at the bottom.
  6. Repeat. He's reset for the next cycle at a tidy profit.

This is exactly what market makers do with shares: accumulate low, mark up, distribute high, mark down. The only place their footprints show is in volume — which is why the volume "quietly drops" at step 3 and spikes on the panic at step 5.

Market makers: the licensed wholesalers

The great irony: outsider insider‑dealing means prison, yet insiders are licensed to do it. Market makers are necessary — they're the market's wholesalers, guaranteeing your order always gets filled. Their edge: they see both sides (supply, demand, inventory) and can set prices.

  • The market isn't fully "rigged" — no single maker could do that.
  • But they exploit windows of opportunity, using any news (relevant or not) to move price.
  • That's why markets move on unrelated events, drop on good news, or rise on bad.
  • Seeing the same supply/demand balance, they act in unison — not overt collusion, just common inventory sense.

They sit in the middle with information you'll never have. The one tool that lets us see inside the market is volume — imperfect (large moves can hide in after‑hours reporting), but the best we've got.

Volume across all markets

Futures — the purest market

Volume shows when a move runs out of steam and when big operators move in/out. Rising price + rising volume = validated up; falling price + rising volume = validated down. Volume is the fuel — if price moves without volume agreeing, an alarm bell rings.

Forex — use tick volume

No true volume is reported, so use tick volume as a proxy — studies suggest it's ~90% representative of real activity. Watch a 233‑tick chart around Non‑Farm Payrolls: bars that took minutes suddenly fire out in seconds.

Three truths about volume

Picture two auctions: an empty provincial saleroom (one bid, price barely moves) vs. a packed city saleroom (price jumps again and again). Activity is volume. From this come three truths:

  1. All volume is relative. You need a yardstick (prior bars) to judge high vs. low. Charts make this instant.
  2. Volume without price is meaningless. Alone it shows only interest — the explosive power of VPA comes from volume + price together.
  3. Time is a key component. The hosepipe & sprinkler: same water (price action), but narrow the aperture (volume) over the same time and pressure rises, so it travels further.

An art, not a science

"…trading and investing is like any other pursuit — the longer you stay at it the more technique you acquire, and anybody who thinks he knows of a short cut that will not involve 'sweat of the brow' is sadly mistaken." — Richard Wyckoff

No free or paid indicator can truly read the price–volume relationship, because it's discretionary and subjective — it won't automate. Mastery takes time (the author spent 6 months studying cash and futures charts side by side), but like the tape readers of old, once learned it's a powerful skill.

Always the wholesaler's perspective

We study volume to follow the insiders — assuming they know where the market's heading. So watch for the two climaxes:

Waterfall down on massive volume → wholesalers buying, retail panic‑selling. An opportunity to buy.

Top of a bull run on sustained high volume → wholesalers selling to euphoric retail buyers.

Throughout, "buying" and "selling" always mean from the wholesaler's side — the order flow we always want to follow.

Reading the two climax charts

Both charts pair a price line with two tall volume bars at the turning point. The trick is that the label describes what the wholesaler is doing — which is the opposite of the panicking public.

Left — "Buying climax": price waterfalls down while volume explodes. The public is panic‑selling at the lows; the wholesalers are on the other side buying everything cheap. That huge volume at the bottom is your cue that a floor is forming — an opportunity to buy.

Right — "Selling climax": price has run up and volume stays huge at the top. The euphoric public is buying the highs; the wholesalers are quietly selling their inventory into that demand. Heavy volume at the top warns the rally is ending.

Key habit to build: whenever you see a volume spike, ask "what are the professionals doing here?" — because the crowd's emotion (panic or euphoria) is usually pointing the wrong way.

Chapter Three — The Right Price

"No price is too low for a bear or too high for a bull."
— Unknown

Price is only half the story

Price is the counterbalance to volume. As Livermore said, "there is nothing new in Wall Street." The one real change in how we read price was the arrival of candlestick charts in the early 1990s.

Today's fad is "Price Action Trading" (PAT) — analysing price with few or no indicators. But imagine telling Livermore, Dow, Wyckoff and Ney that the new ticker would show price but no volume — they'd be dumbfounded. Price is a leading indicator, but on its own it only shows what already happened; volume completes the picture.

Common mistake — "price action alone is enough"

Reading only candles feels clean and modern, but it's like watching that online auction where you see the winning bid yet can't tell if two people or two hundred are bidding. Two identical‑looking candles can mean completely opposite things.

Fix: always pair the candle's shape with its volume bar. The shape gives the direction; the volume tells you whether anyone of size is actually behind it.

🏛️ The LIFFE pit

Pit traders sensed order flow — fear, greed, the "smell of the market." That flow was volume. When trading went electronic, most failed to transition because they lost that tangible feel. Screen volume is its electronic replacement.

💻 The online auction

In a physical saleroom you see the crowd, phone bids and where bidders hesitate. Online you see only price — you can't tell how many bidders exist, or if the auctioneer is taking fake "off‑the‑wall" bids. Trading on price alone is that online auction.

How electronics changed Open, High, Low, Close

In Ney's era markets traded only during a physical session, so the open and close were hugely significant. Then Globex (CME, 1992) made futures trade nearly 24 hours a day.

Because index futures now trade overnight, the cash open is no longer a surprise and old‑style gaps are rare. In a 24‑hour market, one bar's open ≈ the previous bar's close — revealing little. That makes volume even more relevant today.

Reading the timeline

Each dot marks when a major index future launched on the near‑24‑hour Globex system: the platform itself in 1992, then the S&P 500 e‑mini (ES, 1997), the Nasdaq‑100 (NQ, 1999) and the Dow (YM, 2002).

Why it matters for VPA: before this, markets traded only in a set session, so the open and close were huge, information‑rich events. Now that price trades overnight, today's open is basically yesterday's close — the old open/close/gap signals lost most of their punch. Volume stepped in as the reliable signal, which is exactly why this book leans on it.

Anatomy of a candle

Every candle has seven elements: open, high, low, close, the upper and lower wicks, and the spread (the open‑to‑close body). The wicks and spread are the most revealing — especially once validated with volume.

How to read the candle (Fig 3.10)

A candlestick summarises one time period (a minute, a day, a week — whatever the chart is set to). The fat middle is the body; the thin lines above and below are the wicks (also called shadows/tails). Four prices define it:

  1. Open — where price started the period (bottom of the body on this bullish candle).
  2. Close — where price finished (top of the body). Because close is above open, this candle is bullish.
  3. High — the very top of the upper wick, the highest price touched.
  4. Low — the very bottom of the lower wick, the lowest price touched.

The spread is the height of the body (open‑to‑close) — it measures how decisively price moved. The two wicks show ground that was won then given back.

On a down (bearish) candle it's flipped: the open is at the top of the body and the close at the bottom. Colour just tells you up vs. down; the shape is what carries the message.

What's happening inside the candle (Fig 3.11)

A single candle hides a whole battle. This diagram unrolls that battle as a wavy line: price dips below the open (sellers push), then rallies hard above it (buyers push), before settling at the close. The shaded band is the final body — the net result of all that back‑and‑forth.

Here the buyers win: price ends higher than it started, so the finished candle is a bullish body. The wiggles that poked outside the body become the wicks.

The lesson: the completed candle is what matters, not the messy path it took to get there. Two candles can look identical yet have had wildly different journeys — which is exactly why we then add volume to judge how much real effort was behind the outcome.

Wide spread

Strong sentiment — bullish if close > open, bearish if close < open.

Narrow spread

Weak, indecisive sentiment — no strong view either way.

Wicks = change

A wick marks a change of sentiment during the session. No wick means sentiment held firm throughout.

Spread and wicks in plain words

Two quick rules of thumb that you'll use constantly:

Spread (body size) = conviction. A tall body means one side dominated all period (strong sentiment). A tiny body means buyers and sellers were evenly matched (weak, undecided).

Wick = rejection. A wick is price that was reached and then rejected. A long upper wick = buyers tried higher but sellers slapped it back (a hint of weakness). A long lower wick = sellers tried lower but buyers bought it up (a hint of strength). No wicks at all = one side was in total control from start to finish.

The lower‑wick example

Price opens, sellers drive it down, then buyers step in near the low, overwhelm the sellers, and push price back up to close at the open. Two lessons: a complete reversal of sentiment within the session, and a bullish close (price was rising into the finish).

Step by step (Fig 3.12 → Fig 3.14)

  1. Price opens and sellers immediately take charge, dragging it steadily down (the left, falling part of Fig 3.12).
  2. Near the low, buyers step in — selling has run out of willing sellers, so buyers overwhelm them and haul price all the way back up (the right, rising part).
  3. Price closes right back at the open. Net movement for the body: essentially zero.

Collapse that journey into one candle and you get Fig 3.14: a small (or non‑existent) body sitting on top of a long lower wick. This is the classic hammer shape.

Why it's powerful: the long lower tail is the market telling you sellers tried to push lower and completely failed. The tiny body isn't boring — it's the fingerprint of a sentiment reversal from bearish to bullish. Now imagine this appears on high volume: that's proof buyers absorbed a flood of selling — a strong signal a bottom may be in.

The upper‑wick example

The mirror image: buyers push price up, run out of steam at the high, then sellers take control and force price back down to close at the open. A complete reversal from bullish to a bearish close.

Step by step (Fig 3.13 → Fig 3.15)

  1. Price opens and buyers charge, lifting it up to a new high (the rising left part of Fig 3.13).
  2. At the top, buying exhausts — no one left to pay higher prices — and sellers take over, pushing price all the way back down (the falling right part).
  3. Price closes back at the open: again a near‑zero body.

Squeeze that into one candle and you get Fig 3.15: a small body with a long upper wick — the shooting star shape.

Why it matters: the long upper tail shows buyers tried to rally and were rejected. Sentiment flipped from bullish to bearish inside the session. Spot this after a strong run‑up, especially on heavy volume, and it warns the top may be near. Note the caption's point: on a daily or weekly chart this reversal means far more than on a 1‑minute chart, because a full day's worth of participants changed their mind — not just a handful of scalpers.

The same pattern carries far more weight on a daily or weekly chart than on a 1‑ or 5‑minute chart — time matters.

VPA vs. VAP — two views of the same thing

VPA — the outside view

Volume Price Analysis: the relationship between volume and price over the whole completed candle. The big picture.

VAP — the inside view

Volume At Price: where the volume concentrated inside the bar (the volume profile). The detail.

Price action shows the shape of buying and selling — but not its strength or whether it's genuine. That's why PAT tells only half the story: volume completes the picture.

Chapter Four — VPA: First Principles

"Wherever learning curves begin in this mercurial business, they never seem to end."
— John Neff

Six guiding principles

  • 1. Art, not science. VPA is subjective — it can't be automated. Learn it once and use it free for life, in any market or timeframe.
  • 2. Patience. The market is a super‑tanker — it doesn't stop and turn on a dime. A signal is a warning, not an instant trigger; the "mopping up" takes time.
  • 3. It's all relative. Don't obsess over feed accuracy. You compare your volume bars to your own history, so consistency beats precision (imperfect vs. imperfect).
  • 4. Practice makes perfect. ~6 months of daily chart‑watching to become fluent; the skill then works in every timeframe.
  • 5. Technical analysis. VPA is only part of the story — confirm with support/resistance, trends, and patterns for "triangulation."
  • 6. Validation or anomaly. This is all we ever look for: does volume confirm price (continuation), or contradict it (a warning of change)?

Common mistake — "a signal means enter right now"

Beginners see one climax or premier candle and jump straight in, then get stopped out when price wobbles for a few more bars. The market is a super‑tanker — it doesn't stop and reverse instantly.

Fix: treat a signal as a warning to get ready, not a trigger. Wait for confirmation (a test, a cluster of signals, a validated breakout) before committing. Patience is principle #2 for a reason.

The one question: validation or anomaly?

Everything reduces to Wyckoff's effort‑vs‑result law on a single candle. Compare the spread (result) to the volume (effort). If they match, the move is genuine. If they don't, an alarm bell rings.

A wide up‑candle on low volume, or a tiny candle on huge volume, is the market makers showing their hand.

How to use this four‑box grid

Every candle falls into one of four boxes. Across the top: how big the body/spread is (the result). Down the side: how big the volume is (the effort). You simply check whether effort and result match:

  1. Top‑left — Wide + High volume = VALID. A big move backed by big effort. This is honest, genuine price action — trust it to continue.
  2. Bottom‑right — Narrow + Low volume = VALID. A small move on small effort. Also honest — just a quiet, low‑interest period. Nothing wrong.
  3. Bottom‑left — Wide + Low volume = ANOMALY. A big move on almost no effort. That's impossible in a fair market — it means the makers pushed price with little real buying/selling. A fake/trap.
  4. Top‑right — Narrow + High volume = ANOMALY. Enormous effort produced almost no movement. Someone big is absorbing all the buying (or selling) — the classic fingerprint of a top or bottom.

The two green (diagonal) boxes = effort matches result = carry on. The two red boxes = mismatch = alarm bell. This single grid is the engine behind every signal in the rest of the book.

Multiple‑bar validation

The same test applies to groups of candles. In a genuine trend, rising price = rising volume — two levels of validation: each candle, and the trend as a whole.

Reading the two trend charts (Fig 4.14 & 4.15)

Don't just judge one candle — judge the whole run. In each chart, put a ruler along the tops of the price candles, then a second ruler along the tops of the volume bars underneath.

Fig 4.14 (up‑trend): the price candles step higher, and the volume bars grow taller right alongside them. Both slope the same way, so the up‑trend is validated — real buying is fuelling every leg up.

Fig 4.15 (down‑trend): price steps lower while volume again rises. This surprises beginners, but falling prices need effort too: heavy volume on the way down means genuine, aggressive selling. The down‑trend is just as valid.

Takeaway: a healthy trend — up or down — shows volume expanding in the direction of price. You now have two layers of proof: each individual candle, and the trend as a whole.

Multiple‑bar anomalies

Now the warnings. When price keeps trending but volume disagrees, the insiders have stopped participating — they're trapping latecomers while quietly reversing.

Spotting the divergence (Fig 4.16 & 4.17)

These are the mirror image of the previous two — price and volume now point in opposite directions. That gap between them is the warning.

Fig 4.16 (rising price, falling volume): price keeps making higher highs, but each volume bar is smaller than the last. Fewer and fewer people are buying into the rally — the insiders have already sold out and are just letting latecomers lift it. This "over‑bought" state signals hidden weakness: the up‑move is running on fumes and a reversal is near.

Fig 4.17 (falling price, falling volume): price waterfalls lower, but the selling volume drains away. The panic sellers are exhausted — there's almost no one left to sell. This "over‑sold" state signals hidden strength: insiders are quietly buying and a bottom is forming.

Rule to memorise: when price and volume disagree over several bars, the trend is tiring. The direction that runs out of volume is the direction about to fail.

Chapter Five — Building the Picture

"Mistakes are the best teachers. One does not learn from success."
— Mohnish Pabrai

The warehouse cycle: accumulation & distribution

The whole market is the insiders filling and emptying a warehouse, driven by just two emotions in us: fear and greed. Five concepts sit at the heart of VPA: accumulation, distribution, testing, selling climax, buying climax.

Fear empties the public's hands at the bottom; greed fills them at the top. The cycle repeats endlessly, in every market and timeframe.

Reading the two phases (Fig 5.10 & 5.11)

Fig 5.10 — Accumulation: look for a long sideways range near the bottom of a chart, riddled with tall volume bars. Price isn't going anywhere, but the heavy volume tells you a lot is changing hands. That's the insiders quietly buying at "wholesale" prices, using bad news to scare the public into selling to them. It looks boring — that's the disguise.

Fig 5.11 — Distribution: the same sideways‑on‑high‑volume picture, but now at the top after a big rise. Price stalls while heavy volume churns — the insiders selling their inventory at "retail" to a greedy public that's piling in on good news.

The tell for both is identical: price stops trending but volume stays high. Lots of activity with no price progress means ownership is being transferred — from the public to insiders (bottom) or insiders to the public (top).

Testing — the insiders' quality check

Before committing to a move, insiders test whether old supply (or demand) has been absorbed. They nudge price back into a recent battle zone on a small news item and watch the volume:

  • Low‑volume test = GOOD. Few sellers/buyers left → safe to launch the move (a narrow body with a deep wick).
  • High‑volume test = BAD. Old supply/demand returns → the campaign stalls; insiders must "mop up" again and re‑test.

What a "test" looks like (Fig 5.12 & 5.14)

A test is one candle that dips back into a recent battle zone to check if the opposing side has given up. You judge it entirely by the volume on that dip.

  1. Fig 5.12 (after accumulation): price pokes down into the old range, forming a candle with a deep lower wick — but on low volume. Low volume means barely any sellers left. The insiders have their answer: supply is gone, safe to mark price up.
  2. Fig 5.14 (after distribution): the mirror — price pokes up (a candle with an upper wick) on low volume, proving there are few buyers left. Safe for insiders to sell price down.

The wick shows price went there and came straight back — rejected. Pair that rejection with low volume and it's a green light. If the same dip came on high volume, it would mean the old buyers/sellers are still active, and the move would have to wait.

Why markets rise slowly but fall fast

After a crash, insiders rebuild confidence gently (they can't scare everyone away) — so price climbs in "higher highs and higher lows." Once the warehouse is empty, they crash it to refill cheaply. Think helter‑skelter or snakes and ladders: up the stairs, down the elevator.

Why the two halves look so different

The green left half rises in a gentle staircase — small steps up, little pullbacks, higher highs and higher lows. Insiders can't rush this: pushing too fast would spook the public, so they coax confidence in slowly. The red right half drops in one steep plunge, because fear is instant — everyone rushes for the exit at once.

This asymmetry is why the saying goes "up the stairs, down the elevator." Practically: uptrends give you time to get positioned; downtrends punish you fast, so respect topping signals quickly.

Nested cycles across timeframes

These cycles happen in every timeframe — like Russian nesting dolls. An accumulation cycle on a 1‑minute chart sits inside a larger cycle on the 15‑minute, which sits inside the daily, and so on. Watch several timeframes and you'll see them confirm one another — a fast‑chart "microscope" and a slow‑chart "telescope" (Ney).

The climaxes — the "firework show"

Note the author's convention: buying/selling is always from the insiders' side. A selling climax is the insiders selling at the top (end of distribution); a buying climax is them buying at the bottom (end of accumulation).

Reading the climaxes (Fig 5.16 & 5.17)

Remember the author's convention: the name describes the insiders' action, not the public's.

Fig 5.16 — Selling climax at a TOP: after a long rise you see a cluster of candles with long upper wicks on very high volume. Price keeps getting pushed up and slapped back down — that's the public buying the highs while insiders dump everything into them. Once the selling is done, price breaks down. Heavy volume + upper wicks at a high = get ready for a fall.

Fig 5.17 — Buying climax at a BOTTOM: after a long fall you see candles with long lower wicks on very high volume. Price is pushed down and bought straight back up — the public panic‑selling while insiders soak it all up. Then price breaks up. Heavy volume + lower wicks at a low = get ready for a rise.

A climax is rarely a single candle — it's a "firework show" of several high‑volume, long‑wick candles clustered together. The more of them, and the bigger the volume, the more significant the turn.

Chapter Six — The Next Level

"I never argue with the tape."
— Jesse Livermore

Five candle principles

  • 1. The wick is always your first focus — it reveals strength, weakness, or indecision.
  • 2. No wick = strong sentiment in the direction of the close.
  • 3. A narrow body = weak sentiment; a wide body = strong sentiment.
  • 4. The same candle means different things depending on where it sits in the trend.
  • 5. Volume validates price — read the candle first, then check the volume bar.

The three premier candles

These three are your "wake‑up call." Their price action gives the signal; volume gives the strength. One candle = a hint; two or three together = exponentially stronger.

The three shapes and what the volume adds

  1. Shooting star (Fig 6.10) — tiny body, long upper wick. Price shot up and got rejected: weakness. It's always a warning; the volume just tells you how serious — bigger volume = more insiders selling into the high.
  2. Hammer (Fig 6.12) — tiny body, long lower wick. Price was driven down and bought straight back: strength, "hammering out a bottom." On high volume it becomes a buying climax — insiders soaking up the selling.
  3. Long‑legged doji (Fig 6.13) — long wicks both sides, open ≈ close. Pure indecision. Big wild swings that end up nowhere on low volume is an anomaly: violent price with no real effort behind it = the makers running stop‑hunts (very common on NFP news spikes).

Read the shape first (it gives the direction of the signal), then check the volume bar (it gives the strength). One premier candle is a hint; two or three in a row make the signal exponentially stronger.

Stopping & topping‑out volume

The market is a super‑tanker — the brakes take several candles. Watch for the professionals stepping in against the trend:

Also note the hanging man (a hammer shape at a top = weakness) and that VPA doesn't just get you in — it keeps you in a trend and tells you when to get out.

Reading stopping vs. topping (Fig 6.17 & 6.18)

These are climaxes spread over several candles — the "brakes" being applied to a moving market.

Fig 6.17 — Stopping volume (a falling market braking): price has been waterfalling down, then you see a run of candles with deep lower wicks on high volume. The insiders are stepping under the falling price and absorbing all the panic selling. Each lower wick is price being caught and lifted. The fall doesn't reverse instantly — it grinds to a halt over a few bars — then turns up. This is strength.

Fig 6.18 — Topping‑out volume (a rising market braking): the mirror. After a rise, candles arc over with deep upper wicks on high volume — insiders selling into the crowd's greed. Price rolls over into a rounded top. This is weakness.

Because the market is a "super‑tanker," it can't stop on a dime. Don't expect a single reversal candle — look for a cluster of high‑volume, deep‑wick candles that show the professionals leaning against the trend for several bars.

Chapter Seven — Support & Resistance

"Money and markets may never forget, but surely people do."
— Kenneth L. Fisher

Floors and ceilings

If volume and price are the foundations, support & resistance are the walls, floors and ceilings. Markets move up, down, or sideways — and they're sideways ~70% of the time. That congestion builds an invisible floor of support and ceiling of resistance that price bounces between (like ping‑pong).

How to read Fig 7.10

Draw a horizontal line across the lows where price keeps bouncing up — that's support (the floor). Draw another across the highs where price keeps getting knocked back — that's resistance (the ceiling). Price ping‑pongs between the two.

Markets are in this sideways state roughly 70% of the time, so recognising these bands is more useful day‑to‑day than chasing trends. Each touch of the floor is a potential buy, each touch of the ceiling a potential sell — until one of them finally breaks.

Why the barriers form: fear & greed

The levels aren't magic — they're the extremes of fear and greed. Buyers trapped at the top of a wave sell out (relieved) the next time price returns; new buyers enter at the floor. This constant churn packs the zone with weak and strong positions, which become future barriers.

Why the walls exist (Fig 7.11 & 7.12)

Support and resistance aren't magic numbers — they're where crowds of traders got trapped by emotion, and those trapped positions become future barriers.

Fig 7.11 (ceiling at a top): greedy latecomers buy near the high, fearing they'll miss out. Price then falls and leaves them underwater. The next time price climbs back to that level, they sell just to break even — their relief‑selling caps the price and forms resistance.

Fig 7.12 (floor at a bottom): the same in reverse. Panicked sellers dump at the low; when price returns there, fresh buyers step in and the memory of that level creates support.

This is why old highs and lows keep "working" — the barrier is really a memory of trapped money, not a line on the chart.

Spotting congestion live: isolated pivots

The hard part is recognising congestion as it happens. The answer is the isolated pivot — a three‑candle reversal. A pivot high (middle candle has the higher high) marks the ceiling; a pivot low (middle candle has the lower low) marks the floor.

Spotting a pivot (Fig 7.13)

A pivot is the simplest reversal pattern — just three candles:

  1. Pivot high (ceiling marker): the middle candle's high is higher than the candle on either side. Price poked up, found no buyers, and turned back down. That middle peak is a mini‑ceiling.
  2. Pivot low (floor marker): the middle candle's low is lower than both neighbours. Price dipped, found buyers, and turned back up. That middle trough is a mini‑floor.

Why it's useful: you can spot a pivot the moment the third candle closes — you don't have to wait for a whole trend to form. The first pivot high and first pivot low give you the top and bottom lines of a congestion zone in real time.

The breakout — where volume is king

Congestion zones are densely packed, so escaping one needs real volume. A breakout with "clear water" beyond the line on strong volume is genuine. A breakout on low volume is a classic "fake‑out" — the insiders trapping traders on the wrong side.

Remember: the lines are elastic rubber bands, not rods of steel — and the longer the congestion (cause), the bigger the breakout move (effect).

Reading the breakout (Fig 7.16)

  1. Price has been trapped in a congestion band (floor + ceiling) for a while.
  2. A candle pushes through the ceiling and closes with clear water above the line — not just a wick poking through, but a solid close beyond it.
  3. Look straight down at the volume bar. If it's large and rising, real buyers are powering the escape — the breakout is genuine, and you go with it.

The volume check is everything. A breakout on low volume is the classic "fake‑out" — the insiders nudge price just past the line to trigger buy‑stops, then reverse and trap those buyers. No volume = no conviction = don't trust it. And because a congestion zone is packed tight, only real effort can escape it — which is why the longer the squeeze (cause), the bigger the eventual move (effect).

Common mistake — "every breakout is tradable" & "lines are exact"

Two traps here. First, chasing every break of a line — most low‑volume breaks are fake‑outs designed to trap you. Second, treating support/resistance as a razor‑thin price: the lines are elastic rubber bands, not rods of steel, so price often overshoots slightly before respecting a level.

Fix: only trade breakouts confirmed by strong volume with clear water beyond the line, and think of S/R as zones, not exact numbers.

Chapter Eight — Dynamic Trends & Trend Lines

"The loss was not bad luck. It was bad analysis."
— David Einhorn

The problem with traditional trend lines

"Let the trend be your friend" sounds wise but is nearly useless live. Traditional trend lines need three higher highs and higher lows before you can draw them — by which point the trend is already near its climax, and you'd be buying just as the insiders sell.

The trap in the textbook trend (Fig 8.10)

The classic rule says: connect the rising lows with a straight line and you've got an uptrend to buy. To draw that line convincingly you need three higher lows (and higher highs). Count them on the chart.

Here's the problem: by the time three clean higher highs and higher lows exist, the trend is already mature — often near the distribution stage. So the "obvious" entry point is exactly when the insiders are selling to you. The textbook method makes you fashionably late. Chapter 8's fix (dynamic pivot‑based lines) gets you in far earlier.

Dow's own three trend stages — accumulation, trend‑following, distribution — are exactly the insiders' warehouse cycle.

Dynamic trend lines from pivots

The fix: build trend lines dynamically from a volume‑validated breakout, using the same isolated pivots from Chapter 7 as "way points." Each new pivot high above the last, and pivot low above the last, confirms the trend — so you're in near the start, not the end.

When a pivot fails to make a new extreme, the trend is pausing or reversing — exit or re‑assess.

Building the line as you go (Fig 8.14 & 8.15)

Instead of waiting for the whole trend, you use the isolated pivots from Chapter 7 as stepping‑stones and draw the trend line while it forms.

Fig 8.14 (bullish): after a volume‑validated breakout, mark each new pivot low. As long as each pivot low is higher than the last (and pivot highs keep rising too), the uptrend is intact and you're riding it from near the start.

Fig 8.15 (bearish): the mirror — connect lower pivot highs. Each new lower pivot confirms the downtrend continues.

Your exit signal is built in: the moment a pivot fails to make a new extreme (a pivot low that's lower in an uptrend, or highs that stop rising), momentum is gone — time to take profit or re‑assess. This keeps you in for the meat of the move and out before the reversal.

Chapter Nine — Volume At Price (VAP)

"In a bull market it is better to always work on the bull side; in a bear market, on the bear side."
— Charles Dow

VAP: slicing the volume bar open

VPA reads volume over time (the bars along the bottom). VAP reads volume at each price level — a horizontal histogram down the side of the chart. Think of it as taking one volume bar and cutting it open to see where the trading actually happened.

What VAP actually measures (Fig 9.11)

Normal volume bars run along the bottom and answer "how much traded in each time period?" VAP flips the question 90°: horizontal bars run down the side and answer "how much traded at each price level?" A long horizontal bar means price spent a lot of activity there; a short one means price passed through quickly.

Picture taking a single tall volume bar and slicing it into horizontal layers to see which price levels held the most trading. Those fat layers are where the crowd is heavily committed — which is exactly where support and resistance live.

Heavy volume low in a range = buying; heavy volume high = selling. The big VAP bars mark the strongest support and resistance — the invisible barriers, now painted on the chart.

VAP in action

VAP supports VPA — never replaces it. Use VAP to spot the zones, then confirm with classic VPA.

Reading real VAP charts (Fig 9.12 & 9.15)

On both charts the VAP histogram sticks out horizontally from the price action. The rule is simple: the longer the bar, the stronger the barrier at that price.

Fig 9.12 (MSFT 15‑min): the tallest VAP bars mark the heaviest congestion — the strongest support/resistance. The book flags a warning: when a recent zone has already stacked up as much volume as an older zone three times longer, that's a lot of trapped supply building fast — a weak, heavy top.

Fig 9.15 (P&G daily): one extreme VAP bar towers over the rest — a rock‑solid support platform where huge volume changed hands. Above it, a lighter zone offers much weaker support, so price can move through it more easily.

Use VAP to find the important price levels, then switch back to normal VPA (candles + volume‑over‑time) to judge what's happening at those levels. VAP shows you where the walls are; VPA tells you whether they're about to break.

Chapter Ten — VPA Examples

"Two key factors: correct action and patience. These you must supply yourself."

The same method, every market

Reading a chart is a running commentary in your head — candle by candle, asking "validation or anomaly?" The examples below are stocks, ETFs, forex and futures, on timeframes from tick to weekly. The analysis is identical in every case.

Reading the chart like a story (Fig 10.10)

This is everything from earlier chapters strung together into one running commentary. Left to right:

  1. Shooting star — after a rise, a long upper wick warns of weakness; the rally is tiring.
  2. Stopping volume — as price falls, a cluster of high‑volume, deep‑lower‑wick candles shows insiders catching the sell‑off. The decline brakes.
  3. Hammer — a long lower wick confirms buyers have taken control at the low: a bottom is forming.

No single candle told the whole story — the sequence did. This candle‑by‑candle narration ("is this validation or anomaly?") is exactly how you should read any chart.

Across the markets

The big picture

Zoom right out and the anomalies leap off the page. Your eye is drawn to the extreme volume — that's where the insiders acted.

Why zooming out works (Fig 10.22)

On a long weekly chart your eye is instantly pulled to the tallest volume bars — and that's precisely where the insiders were most active. Here the accumulation zone (a block of heavy volume near a low) jumps out as obvious buying.

Just as important is what's missing: there's no selling climax (no heavy‑volume topping) yet, which tells you the insiders haven't sold — so the trend is still intact. Big players must trade in size, and size shows up as volume, so on a wide view their footprints are impossible to hide.

Chapter Eleven — Putting It All Together

"The market does not run on chance or luck. Like the battlefield, it runs on probabilities and odds."
— David Dreman

Trade with multiple timeframes

Use three charts: a slower benchmark (the dominant trend), your primary trading chart in the middle, and a faster one for fine detail. A signal ripples from fast → primary → slow like ripples in a pond. Trading with the dominant trend is lower‑risk; against it is counter‑trend (higher risk, shorter hold).

Why three timeframes (Fig 11.12)

The same price action looks different depending on the chart's speed. One clean shooting star on the 30‑min chart is the same price move as 2 candles on the 15‑min and 6 candles on the 5‑min — but on the fast chart it's messy and easy to miss, while on the slow chart it's a single obvious warning.

So use three charts together: a slow one to see the dominant trend and the clean signals, your primary chart to trade from, and a fast one for precise entries. A real signal will ripple across all three. Trading with the slow‑chart trend is lower risk; trading against it is counter‑trend — higher risk and hold it for less time.

Common mistake — trading one timeframe in isolation

A perfect buy signal on your 5‑minute chart is a losing trade if the daily chart is in a strong downtrend — you're fighting the dominant flow. Small‑chart signals against the big‑chart trend fail far more often.

Fix: always check the slower benchmark chart first. Only take counter‑trend trades knowingly, small, and briefly — and prefer signals that line up across all three timeframes.

Reliable congestion patterns

These patterns form in congestion and, once broken on volume, spawn trends or reversals. Remember cause & effect — the longer the pattern, the bigger the move.

Recognising the five patterns

  1. Falling triangle (Fig 11.13): a flat floor with a sloping‑down ceiling (lower highs). Buyers keep weakening → usually breaks down.
  2. Rising triangle (Fig 11.14): a flat ceiling with a sloping‑up floor (higher lows). Buyers keep pressing → usually breaks up.
  3. Pennant (Fig 11.15): both lines converge into a tight point — a "coiled spring." Expect an explosive breakout, but the pattern alone doesn't tell you the direction; wait for the volume‑backed break.
  4. Triple top (Fig 11.16): price hits the same ceiling and is rejected three times. Sellers keep winning there → reversal down risk.
  5. Triple bottom (Fig 11.17): the mirror — support holds three times → potential reversal up.

In every case the pattern only "fires" when price breaks out on strong volume — a low‑volume break is a fake‑out. And remember cause & effect: the longer the pattern took to form, the bigger the move that follows.

Chapter Twelve — The Next Generation

"My biggest winnings were not in dollars but in the intangibles: I had been right… and followed a clear‑cut plan."
— Jesse Livermore

Newer volume tools

The author closes with emerging techniques she hadn't traded herself, but wanted to flag:

What the three new tools do

  1. Equivolume (Fig 12.10): throws away the time axis and makes each box's width equal its volume. High‑volume periods become fat boxes, low‑volume ones thin. Volume becomes instantly visual — but the author's caveat is that dropping time loses Wyckoff's cause & effect.
  2. Candlevolume (Fig 12.11): a hybrid that keeps proper candlesticks (with wicks) but stretches each candle's width to match its volume. You get VPA's candle shapes and a sense of volume in one glance.
  3. Delta volume (Fig 12.12): splits each bar's volume into aggressive buys (trades hitting the ask) minus aggressive sells (trades hitting the bid). Positive delta = buyers dominating, negative = sellers. Cumulative delta adds it up through the session to show the running balance of power.

The author flags these as promising but secondary. The closing message stands: plain price + volume is still the core method — these are extra lenses, not replacements. It remains an art, but "there is no hiding place" for the insiders.

The closing message: volume and price together are the only indicators that truly reveal market sentiment and the insiders' activity. No software can do it for you — it's an art that takes patience and practice. But once learned, "there is no hiding place."

Test yourself

Read each scenario, decide your answer, then click to reveal. If you can breeze through these, you've understood the book.

1. Price makes a new high, but the volume bar is smaller than the last few. Validation or anomaly?
Anomaly. Rising price on falling volume is "over‑bought" — fewer buyers each push, so insiders have likely stopped participating. Expect a stall or reversal; don't chase the high.
2. A market falls hard, then prints a candle with a long lower wick on very high volume. What's happening?
Stopping volume / a buying climax. The long lower wick shows sellers were overwhelmed and price was bought straight back up; the high volume is insiders absorbing the panic. Strength — a bottom may be forming.
3. Price breaks above a congestion ceiling, but the volume on the breakout candle is low. Do you buy?
No. A breakout on low volume is a classic fake‑out — the makers nudge price past the line to trigger buy‑stops, then reverse. A real breakout needs strong, rising volume.
4. A wide, bullish candle appears — but on unusually low volume. What is it?
A trap (anomaly). A big result with almost no effort can't be genuine buying; it's price being pushed to lure in eager traders. Especially common at the open. Stay out.
5. After a long rise, you see several candles with long upper wicks on heavy volume. What do you prepare for?
A top / reversal down (selling climax or topping‑out volume). The upper wicks show buyers being rejected while insiders sell into the crowd's greed. Get ready to exit longs.
6. Price dips back into a recent range on very low volume, then turns up. Good or bad sign for a rally?
Good. That's a successful test — low volume proves few sellers remain, so supply has been absorbed. It's a green light for the move higher.
7. On the VAP histogram, one horizontal bar is far longer than the rest. What does it mark?
The strongest support/resistance. A long VAP bar means huge volume traded at that price — a heavily defended level where price is likely to react.
8. Enormous volume, but the candle body is tiny (narrow spread). What's the market telling you?
Absorption — a likely top or bottom. Massive effort produced almost no movement, so someone big is soaking up all the buying (or selling). Effort and result disagree = reversal warning.
